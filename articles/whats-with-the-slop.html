What’s with the slop?

Everyone talks about AI slop. But how did this word become so mainstream?

By Amay Prabhu·4 min read

Somewhere in the last two years, a small sparkle icon appeared on almost every button we use. On the email that was being drafted. On the search bar. On the photo editor, the spreadsheet, and even the food delivery app. Nobody asked for it, and nobody voted on it. It simply arrived, with AI now sitting inside tools that we had been using for years.

As companies continued with their AI initiatives, netizens have started expressing varying opinions about the use of AI and the outputs generated by these models. One word that has slipped into everyday lingo without anyone teaching it to us is AI slop.

Now, this piece doesn’t focus on the technical nuances of AI slop. We believe that’s best left to the experts in the domain. But what’s interesting to understand is how this word gained all the traction, to the point where it no longer needs explaining to anyone.

A tale of two slops

Before jumping into AI slop, let’s simply understand what slop means. Imagine you are pouring yourself a cup of coffee and accidentally overfill the cup to its capacity. The coffee spills over and slops onto the table. That is the meaning of the word in one sense, rather an old one. The word has existed since the 1300s, with widely different meanings.

The early definitions suggest that slop meant a mudhole or a puddle*. By the 1650s, it meant semi-liquid food. By 1815, it meant household waste. Like slime, sludge, and muck, slop has the wet sound of something you don’t want to touch.

But here’s the thing. There is a second slop too that is even older. This one was stitched rather than spilled. In the late 1300s, slop meant a loose outer garment, something you slip on. Eventually, slops meant ready-made clothing produced in bulk, and a slop-shop was where you bought it!*

Put together, the two slop definitions give us waste on one side and mass production on the other. Does this remind you of anything AI has been accused of lately?

Frenzy on the forums

In his bestselling book Contagious, Jonah Berger looks at why some ideas take off, and others don’t*. A lot of it boils down to how something makes people feel. What travels is what carries heat. A feeling of excitement, humour, and above all, irritation. Slop had all three, and it had them long before most people heard the word.

By most accounts, it was already circulating on the forums by 2022, when the first public image generators arrived and users on 4chan, Hacker News, and YouTube began using it for what came out of them*. Other words were doing the rounds at the time, including AI garbage and AI pollution, but none of them landed the way slop did. Slop continued to stay niche until it started showing up in the one place everybody looks: searching the internet.

In the pre-AI era, a Google search would list the most relevant links to the page that contained the information that was being searched for. The decision lay with the user on which search result they found to be the most relevant and wished to proceed with. Now, with Gemini embedded into Google’s search functionality, the user sees a summarised version of the information that the model retrieves by crawling across numerous pages on the web. During its initial days, this AI-powered search gave users a lot of irrelevant information, as highlighted in a tweet from May 2024*.

That was the moment everyone else caught up and had an example of their own. Within days, popular British programmer Simon Willison was arguing on his blog that slop should become the standard term for unwanted AI content, the way spam did for unwanted email*.

A title-winning episode

By the end of 2025, slop was no longer slang. In December, Merriam-Webster named it word of the year, defining it as low-quality digital content, usually churned out in bulk by AI*.

What makes their announcement worth reading is the list of examples that came with it. It is a strange list. At one end there are things that can actually hurt society, like cheap propaganda and fake news that is convincing enough to be perceived as real. On the other end of the spectrum, we have talking cats. Somewhere in between is the version of slop that most of us have experienced at work. A deck, a summary, a status update that looks finished. It is structured and appears to be useful. It just doesn’t convey anything concrete, and someone still has to go back and ask what its core purpose was.

A word that stretches across a state propaganda campaign, a wasted afternoon at work, and a piano-playing cat is not really a word about quality at all. It is a word about how something was made, and how little there is underneath.

This is what makes the sentiment behind it worth sitting with. Slop is not the word of a society that has rejected AI content. It is a word that people consume, resent consuming, and finally have a way to say so.

Sifting through the slop

This brings us back to where the word came from. Nobody made this word up. It was already there, waiting with its varying historical definitions. When enough people needed a way to describe what was appearing on their screens, they reached for something that the language had been sitting on for six hundred years.

Slop is not a verdict on the technology. Every instance of slop reached the public the same way. Somebody generated it. Somebody chose not to read it. Somebody sent it out anyway. The tool only amplified the situation and made the volume possible. But leaning on it too heavily and not looking closely enough made it public.

The debate was never really about being for or against AI. Framing the question in that manner has been convenient for everyone and useful to nobody. What sits underneath it is a more serious question: Whether the person operating the tool has done the thinking, or has handed it over entirely and forwarded whatever came back. These are genuinely useful tools, and pretending otherwise is its own kind of dishonesty. But using them well asks something of us that using them badly does not.

The word arrived because we needed it. What we do with it now, and what we are willing to put our name on, is still ours to decide.
